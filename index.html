What's actually growing here

Physarum polycephalum is a single cell — no brain, no neurons, not even a nervous system — and it happens to be remarkably good at building efficient networks. Give it two food sources, and it grows a web of tube-like veins connecting them, then quietly dismantles every branch that isn't pulling its weight until what's left is close to the shortest useful path. That's the exact two-step this site is modeling: grow first, prune second — just against real street data instead of a petri dish.

01

Lean toward what's working

Every growing tip is more likely to head toward the goal than to wander off — but it's a lean, not a rule. Enough randomness survives that it doesn't walk in a straight line and miss a better route sitting right next to it.

02

Reward traffic, starve silence

Any path carrying real flow gets reinforced and thickens. Anything sitting idle — a dead end, a route nobody needs — shrinks and disappears. That's the retraction you watch happen once growth finishes.

03

Never fully commit

A pure "chase the best option so far" strategy gets stuck on the first decent answer it finds. Slime mold keeps exploring even after finding something that works, which is exactly why it doesn't settle for mediocre.

The experiment that made people take this seriously

In 2010, researchers laid oat flakes on a map of Tokyo, one flake per major rail station, and let a Physarum colony loose on it. Left alone, it grew a network connecting every station — and when compared against Tokyo's actual, human-engineered rail system, the two were startlingly close: nearly the same total cost, matching average travel efficiency between stations, and a real (if slightly lower) tolerance for a single link going down. An organism with zero engineering training landed within a few percentage points of a century of civil engineering.
