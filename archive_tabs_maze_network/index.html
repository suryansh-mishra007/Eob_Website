Elements of Biology — Biomimicry in Tech

A brainless organism
can solve a maze — and design a network.

Physarum polycephalum, a single-celled slime mold, is famous for two experiments. Dropped into a maze with food at both ends, it finds the shortest route through, even when several paths exist (Nakagaki, Yamada & Tóth, Nature, 2000). Given food placed at the locations of Tokyo's cities, it grew a network of tubes that closely mirrored the real Tokyo rail system — without a brain, a map, or any planning at all (Tero et al., Science, 2010). Researchers wrote down exactly what rule the organism follows — a feedback loop between how much a tube carries and how thick it grows — as a real system of equations (Tero, Kobayashi & Nakagaki, 2007). This project runs that literal model, not a stylized animation of it, in two interactive demos below.

The Organism Behind the Idea

Physarum polycephalum has no neurons, no central nervous system, and no eyes — just a single sprawling cell that feeds by extending tube-like veins outward in every direction. When it finds food, it reinforces the tube that led there; tubes that lead nowhere useful are starved and retracted. The result is a constant tug-of-war between reinforcement and decay.

In 2000, researchers let the mold grow through a maze with food at the entrance and exit; of the several possible routes, it reliably thinned down to the shortest one. In 2010, a follow-up team placed oat flakes on a map in the positions of cities around Tokyo, then let the mold grow across it. Within a day, it had pruned itself down to a network strikingly similar to the real Tokyo rail system — a system engineers spent decades refining. The mold had no way of "knowing" the maze or the map. It simply followed a local rule, over and over, and an efficient global answer fell out of it.

That local rule has an actual mathematical form, published in the same research group's earlier paper. Every tube (i,j) has a conductivity Dij, and the whole network at any instant obeys Kirchhoff's current law and Poiseuille flow — exactly like a circuit or a plumbing system:

Qij = (Dij / Lij)(pi − pj)

where Q is the flux through a tube, L its length, and p the pressure at each junction, solved so that flow is conserved at every node. Then, every tube adapts to how much flow it's actually carrying:

dDij/dt = f(|Qij|) − Dij

f is an increasing, saturating function of flow — busy tubes get reinforced, idle ones simply decay back toward zero because upkeep isn't free. That's the entire rule. No planning, no comparing routes, no picking a winner — an answer falls directly out of solving those two equations forward in time. Both live demos below run precisely that: no agents, no branching probabilities, nothing hand-tuned to "look like" growth. Every possible connection starts with equal conductivity, gets solved as a live circuit each instant, and visibly thins to whichever tubes the equations keep reinforcing.

The feedback loop

  1. 01 Every possible connection starts as a tube with equal conductivity — the whole structure is "alive" at once, like the real organism first covering the whole plate.
  2. 02 At every instant, the network is solved like a circuit (Kirchhoff's current law) to find how much flow passes through every tube.
  3. 03 Each tube's conductivity adapts toward that flow — busier tubes thicken, idle ones decay.
  4. 04 Repeating that loop is the entire algorithm: the surviving thick tubes, once it settles, are the answer the model predicts.

Mapping Biology to This Model

Slime mold

Starts by covering the whole area it has access to with a dense, uniform mesh of protoplasmic tubes.

This model

Every possible connection starts as a tube with equal conductivity Dij — the whole structure is "alive" at once.

Slime mold

Obeys Poiseuille flow through its tubes — flux is proportional to conductivity and pressure difference, conserved at every junction.

This model

Solves that exact same circuit — Kirchhoff's current law over the conductance-weighted graph — at every instant, via conjugate gradient.

Slime mold

Reinforces tubes carrying more flow, with diminishing returns; a tube costs upkeep, so idle ones decay.

This model

Runs dD/dt = f(|Q|) − D on every edge every step — the identical reinforcement/decay rule, not an approximation of it.

Slime mold

Ends up with only the tubes that were actually carrying flow, everything else physically gone.

This model

Tube width on screen IS conductivity, live — idle tubes visibly thin to nothing as the same equation that thickens the survivors plays out.

References

The model implemented on this page — not a metaphor for it — comes directly from these papers. The equations in The Science section above and the code in physarum-solver.js follow them directly; anywhere this site's implementation makes its own parameter choice (initial conductivity, integration step size, the specific decay floor) rather than reproducing a number reported in a paper, that's called out in the code comments rather than presented as the paper's own value.

  1. Tero, A., Kobayashi, R., & Nakagaki, T. (2007). A mathematical model for adaptive transport network in path finding by true slime mold. Journal of Theoretical Biology, 244(4), 553–564. doi:10.1016/j.jtbi.2006.07.015 — the source of the core model: Kirchhoff's current law over a conductance-weighted graph, plus the dD/dt = f(|Q|) − D reinforcement/decay rule used by both live models on this page.
  2. Tero, A., Takagi, S., Saigusa, T., Ito, K., Bebber, D. P., Fricker, M. D., Yumiki, K., Kobayashi, R., & Nishiguchi, T. (2010). Rules for biologically inspired adaptive network design. Science, 327(5964), 439–442. doi:10.1126/science.1177894 — the Tokyo rail-network replication, and the source of the single-source/evenly-split-sinks simplification used by the Network Builder demo.
  3. Nakagaki, T., Yamada, H., & Tóth, Á. (2000). Maze-solving by an amoeboid organism. Nature, 407(6803), 470. doi:10.1038/35035159 — the original single-source/single-sink maze experiment the Maze Solver demo directly recreates the mathematics of.
